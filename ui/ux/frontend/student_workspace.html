[Section 1 • Fiscal Insolvency]: The French Revolution erupted primarily because the Crown was rendered insolvent after funding the Seven Years' War and the American War of Independence. By 1788, over 50% of the royal budget was consumed strictly by debt interest payments. As Controller-General Calonne admitted, the royal treasury could no longer sustain expenditures through emergency short-term loans.

[Section 2 • Three Estates Inequity]: Compounding this royal insolvency, under the Ancien Régime, the First Estate (Clergy) and Second Estate (Nobility) were legally exempt from direct taxes, leaving the Third Estate—comprising 97% of the French population—to shoulder the entire burden of the taille. As Arthur Young observed in 1789, this institutional privilege concentrated royal fiscal pressure entirely upon peasant agricultural labor, rendering tax reform politically impossible without convoking the Estates-General.
